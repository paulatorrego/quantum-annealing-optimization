# 5. Quantum Annealing

## 5.1 From Ising optimization to a quantum Hamiltonian

After mapping a binary optimization problem to

$$
H_{\mathrm{Ising}}(\mathbf{s})=
\sum_i h_i s_i+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j,
$$

replace the classical spins by Pauli-$Z$ operators:

$$
s_i\rightarrow\hat\sigma_i^z.
$$

The problem Hamiltonian becomes

$$
\hat H_P=
\sum_i h_i\hat\sigma_i^z+
\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\hat\sigma_i^z\hat\sigma_j^z.
$$

Its computational-basis ground states encode solutions of the optimization problem.

## 5.2 Driver Hamiltonian

A common transverse-field driver is

$$
\hat H_B=-\sum_i\hat\sigma_i^x.
$$

Its ground state is

$$
|+\rangle^{\otimes N},
$$

where

$$
|+\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Thus

$$
|+\rangle^{\otimes N}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^N}}\sum_{\mathbf z}|\mathbf z\rangle.
$$

## 5.3 Annealing Hamiltonian

A standard model is

$$
\hat H(s)=A(s)\hat H_B+B(s)\hat H_P,
\qquad
s=\frac{t}{t_f},
\qquad
0\leq s\leq1.
$$

Initially the driver dominates, while near the end the problem Hamiltonian dominates:

$$
A(0)\gg B(0),
\qquad
A(1)\ll B(1).
$$

## 5.4 Adiabatic picture

Let

$$
\hat H(t)|\psi_n(t)\rangle=E_n(t)|\psi_n(t)\rangle.
$$

The instantaneous gap is

$$
\Delta(t)=E_1(t)-E_0(t).
$$

The minimum gap is

$$
\Delta_{\min}=\min_t\Delta(t).
$$

The adiabatic intuition is that slower evolution helps the system remain near the instantaneous ground state when the relevant spectral gaps are sufficiently large. Quantitative adiabatic conditions also depend on matrix elements of $d\hat H/dt$, so the gap alone does not determine runtime.

## 5.5 Quantum tunneling

Classical local search may need to climb an energy barrier. Quantum dynamics can allow transitions through barriers via tunneling. This is a mechanism, not a guarantee of superior optimization performance.

## 5.6 Real hardware is an open system

A real annealer interacts with its environment. Thermal transitions, control errors, decoherence, finite annealing time and readout errors can influence the output.

The final state should therefore not be described simply as the ground state with probability one.

A useful conceptual effect is freeze-out: at some stage, the system may cease to track the instantaneous equilibrium or dynamical state effectively.

## 5.7 Sampling

An annealer returns samples

$$
\mathbf{s}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{s}^{(R)}.
$$

For each sample,

$$
E_r=H_{\mathrm{Ising}}(\mathbf{s}^{(r)}).
$$

Useful quantities include

$$
E_{\min}=\min_rE_r,
$$

and, for constrained problems,

$$
p_{\mathrm{feasible}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{feasible}}}{R}.
$$

If the optimum is known,

$$
p_{\mathrm{success}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{optimal}}}{R}.
$$

## 5.8 Annealing time versus reads

Annealing time controls the duration of an individual anneal. Number of reads controls how many samples are collected. Increasing reads does not make an individual anneal more adiabatic.

## 5.9 Practical workflow

$$
\boxed{
\text{problem}
\rightarrow
\text{binary encoding}
\rightarrow
\text{QUBO/BQM}
\rightarrow
\text{Ising}
\rightarrow
\text{embedding}
\rightarrow
\text{annealing}
\rightarrow
\text{samples}
\rightarrow
\text{post-processing}
}
$$

Each stage has its own possible errors and overheads.

## 5.10 Quantum versus simulated annealing

Simulated annealing uses classical stochastic dynamics controlled by temperature. Quantum annealing uses quantum dynamics generated by a time-dependent Hamiltonian, together with environmental effects in real hardware.

Neither mechanism alone implies a universal performance advantage. Comparisons require controlled problem families, baselines, budgets and metrics.

## 5.11 What a serious experiment should report

At minimum:

- problem family and instance;
- QUBO/Ising convention;
- coefficient scaling;
- embedding information;
- annealing time;
- number of reads;
- post-processing;
- objective and feasibility metrics;
- hardware/software versions.

## 5.12 Summary

The essential chain is

$$
\boxed{
\text{QUBO}
\rightarrow
\text{Ising}
\rightarrow
\text{problem Hamiltonian}
\rightarrow
\text{annealing Hamiltonian}
\rightarrow
\text{samples}
}.
$$

The samples must always be decoded and validated against the original optimization problem.
